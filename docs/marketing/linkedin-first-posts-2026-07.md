# Supplier Reply Review 第一批 LinkedIn 帖子：2026-07

这些帖子用于第一轮 MVP 验证。目标是获得真实买家对话，不硬广、不承诺结果、不写 supplier verification。

## Post 1: Fast reply vs clear reply

A fast supplier reply is not always a clear supplier reply.

When buyers are sourcing from China, speed can feel reassuring. The supplier replies quickly. They say “yes, we can do it.” They quote a sample fee. They say production is no problem.

But before paying a sample fee or deposit, I would separate the reply into three parts:

1. What did the supplier actually confirm?
2. What is still unclear?
3. What should be asked next in writing?

A reply can be fast and still miss key details:

- exact product version
- material or finish
- sample scope
- packaging
- payment account name
- whether bulk production will match the sample

This does not mean the supplier is bad. It means the buyer should not confuse speed with clarity.

Before moving forward, ask for the missing details in writing.

## Post 2: Before paying deposit

Before paying a Chinese supplier deposit, I would check five things first.

Not because every supplier is risky.

Because the deposit usually happens before the buyer has full visibility.

The five checks:

1. Does the payment account name match the invoice or company name?
2. Is the exact product version written down?
3. Are packaging and logo details confirmed?
4. Does the lead time mean production time or delivery time?
5. Are sample-to-bulk differences clearly stated?

None of these checks can guarantee the order.

But they can reduce avoidable confusion before money moves.

The key question is not “Can I trust this supplier?”

The more useful first question is:

What has this supplier actually confirmed?

## Post 3: Factory videos

Factory videos can be useful, but they are limited.

If a supplier sends a polished video showing a gate, showroom, machines, and workers, that is still only a signal.

It does not automatically prove:

- the supplier owns the factory
- your product will be made there
- the sample will match bulk production
- packaging and labels are handled in-house
- the order will be delivered correctly

On a video call, I would ask to see things connected to the actual order:

- similar products
- sample room
- packaging area
- labels or cartons if relevant
- the production flow for that product category
- what is outsourced

The goal is not to turn a video call into a formal audit.

The goal is simpler:

reduce confusion before the next payment or production step.

## Post 4: Sample vs bulk

A sample is not the same as bulk approval.

This is one of the most common sourcing misunderstandings I see.

A sample can help confirm appearance, feel, size, or workmanship.

But unless the scope is written clearly, the sample may not confirm:

- bulk material
- packaging
- logo method
- color tolerance
- production process
- final lead time
- carton labels

Before paying for a sample, I would ask:

What exactly is this sample meant to prove?

Then ask the supplier to confirm:

1. what is included in the sample
2. what may change in bulk production
3. what must be reconfirmed before mass order

A sample is useful.

But unclear sample scope can create false confidence.

## Post 5: Low quote

A low supplier quote is not automatically a red flag.

The problem is a low quote with unclear scope.

If one supplier is much cheaper than the others, I would not reject it immediately. I would first ask what the price actually includes.

Check:

- product version
- material
- finish
- packaging
- logo method
- MOQ
- lead time
- shipping terms
- extra costs

Sometimes the lower quote is valid.

Sometimes it excludes details that other suppliers included.

Sometimes it is for a different product version.

The buyer’s job is not to guess.

Ask the supplier to make the assumptions visible.
