## 1. LinkedIn Long Post

Before a first order from China, the dangerous signal is often not one giant red flag.

It is a chain of small unclear details.

Each one may look manageable alone.

Together, they can make the order hard to control after the deposit is paid.

I would slow down if I see these signals:

1. The supplier says yes to everything
If every requirement is accepted immediately, ask how they will actually meet the material, packaging, tolerance, lead time, and customization requirements.

2. The price is much lower, but the explanation is vague
A lower price may be real, but the supplier should explain what changed: material, packaging, process, quantity, included items, or excluded costs.

3. Payment details do not match the supplier identity
If the quotation company, invoice company, email signature, and bank account name do not align, ask for a written explanation before payment.

4. Sample and bulk details are not written down
If the sample terms, packaging, customization, and bulk production assumptions are not clear, the buyer may approve one thing and receive another.

5. Lead time keeps changing
Changing lead time is not always a disaster, but unclear reasons are a warning sign. Ask what step is causing the change.

The issue is not to panic over every imperfect answer.

The issue is whether the supplier can clarify the uncertainty before money moves.

A first order becomes easier to manage when the missing details are visible before deposit, not after production starts.

## 2. Reddit Answer

I would slow down if the supplier is pushing for a deposit before the basic details are written clearly.

Some things I would treat as warning signs:

- they say yes to every requirement without asking technical questions
- price is much lower than others, but they cannot explain why
- payment account name does not match the supplier company name
- sample terms are vague
- packaging details are missing
- lead time changes every time you ask
- they avoid questions about material, size, finish, tolerances, or production steps
- invoice, company name, email signature, and bank details do not line up

One red flag does not always mean the supplier is bad. But a group of vague answers is a reason to slow down.

Example: if the supplier says "30% deposit now, we confirm packaging later," I would not pay yet if packaging matters to the order. Get packaging type, carton details, labels, logo method, and any extra cost written before payment.

Also, very low price is not always fake. It may be lower because material, packaging, QC standard, shipping term, or included accessories are different. Ask what exactly is included and excluded.

## 3. Quora Answer

Short answer: the biggest red flags before a first order are vague answers, inconsistent company/payment details, unclear sample or bulk terms, and pressure to pay before important details are confirmed.

Here are the red flags I would watch:

1. The supplier says yes to everything
If every requirement is accepted immediately, ask for details. A real answer should explain material, packaging, lead time, customization, tolerance, or process limits.

2. The price is much lower but not explained
A low price is not automatically bad. It becomes a concern when the supplier cannot explain what is included, excluded, changed, or simplified.

3. Payment account does not match the supplier company name
For example, the quotation comes from one company, but the bank account belongs to a different company with no written explanation. Ask for clarification before sending money.

4. Sample terms are unclear
Ask whether the sample is stock, similar, modified, or custom. Also ask what may change before bulk production.

5. Bulk production details are not written down
Material, size, color, finish, packaging, logo, lead time, MOQ, and payment terms should be written clearly before the first order.

6. Supplier pushes deposit before details are confirmed
If the supplier says "pay first, we confirm later," slow down. Confirm the important details in writing first.

Example:
If the supplier company name is "ABC Industrial Co., Ltd." but the payment account belongs to a different company and the supplier gives only a vague answer like "same boss," I would ask for a written explanation and matching invoice documents before paying.

These red flags do not prove that an order will fail. They show where the buyer should ask more questions before moving forward.

## 5. Short FAQ Block

### 1. Is a very low price always a red flag?

No. A low price is not automatically a problem. It becomes a red flag when the supplier cannot explain what changed in material, packaging, process, included items, or order terms.

### 2. What if a supplier says yes to every requirement?

Slow down and ask how each requirement will be met. A useful supplier answer should explain specific material, process, packaging, lead time, customization, or limitation details.

### 3. Is a mismatched payment account dangerous?

It is a serious signal that needs clarification. If the payment account name does not match the supplier company name, ask for a written explanation and matching invoice details before paying.

### 4. Should I pay a deposit before all details are confirmed?

Avoid paying while key details are still vague. Product version, packaging, sample terms, bulk assumptions, payment account, and lead time should be clear enough before deposit.

### 5. What should I do if supplier answers are vague?

Ask for one written summary of product details, included items, excluded items, sample basis, packaging, lead time, payment details, and open questions. If the supplier still avoids specifics, slow down.
