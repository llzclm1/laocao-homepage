## 1. LinkedIn Long Post

A supplier calling itself a "manufacturer" is not enough.

For buyers, the more useful question is:

What does this supplier actually control?

A supplier may be a real factory, a trading company, or something in between. That is not always the problem. The problem starts when the supplier hides what it controls and what it outsources.

Before treating a supplier as a factory, I would check four things:

1. Name consistency
Does the company name match across the business license, invoice, quotation, website, email signature, and payment account?

2. Product focus
Does the supplier focus on a believable product category, or does the range cover too many unrelated products?

3. Technical answers
Can they answer product-specific questions about materials, tolerances, packaging, tooling, defects, or production steps?

4. Live factory context
Can they show a short live video connected to your product category, not just a showroom, office, gate, or generic workshop?

Factory photos and videos can help.

But they cannot prove everything.

They are useful signals when they match the supplier's product claims, quotation details, and technical answers.

The goal is not to accuse every trading company. Some trading companies are useful and honest.

The real issue is hidden control: when a supplier claims to be the factory but cannot explain what it actually makes, manages, or outsources.

## 2. Reddit Answer

I would check what they actually control, not just whether they say "we are manufacturer."

A few things I would look at:

- Does the company name match across the quotation, invoice, website, email signature, and payment account?
- Is their product range believable, or are they selling 20 unrelated categories?
- Can they answer technical questions about your exact product, not just give generic sales replies?
- Can they show a short live video of the relevant production area, sample room, packaging area, or machines connected to your product?
- Do their photos/videos match the products they are quoting?

I would also ask direct questions during a video call:

- Which steps are done in-house?
- Which steps are outsourced?
- Can you show the current material, semi-finished goods, packaging, or similar product?
- Who handles tooling or customization?
- What part of the process is most likely to affect lead time?

Trading company is not always bad. Sometimes a trading company is more organized than a small factory.

The problem is when they hide it. If they claim to be the factory but cannot show product-specific control or explain the process, I would slow down.

A video or photo will not prove everything. It just gives you signals to compare against their claims.

## 3. Quora Answer

Short answer: do not rely only on the supplier saying "we are a factory" or on platform badges. Check whether their company details, product focus, technical answers, and visible factory context all point in the same direction.

Here are the checks I would use:

1. Check company name consistency
Compare the business license, invoice, quotation, payment account, email signature, and website. If the names are different, ask why before moving forward.

2. Check the product range
A supplier claiming to be a factory but selling many unrelated categories may still be a trader, group company, or sourcing office. That is not automatically bad, but it needs explanation.

3. Check product-specific answers
Ask about materials, process steps, tooling, packaging, tolerances, defects, or lead time risks. A supplier with real control should usually answer beyond broad phrases like "high quality" or "no problem."

4. Ask for a short live video
Ask to see the production area, sample room, packaging area, warehouse, machines, or similar products related to your category. Avoid relying only on office or showroom videos.

5. Ask what is in-house and what is outsourced
This is often more useful than asking "Are you a factory?" A clear supplier can explain which steps they control and which steps are handled by partners.

6. Compare all signals together
Photos, documents, answers, payment account, and quotation details should make sense together. One signal alone is not enough.

Example:
If a supplier claims to be a factory for stainless steel kitchenware but their catalog also includes electronics, toys, furniture, and cosmetics, I would ask what they actually manufacture in-house and what they source from other factories.

This check cannot prove that a supplier will perform well. It only helps you understand whether the supplier's factory claim matches visible and written signals.

## 5. Short FAQ Block

### 1. Can a trading company still be useful?

Yes. A trading company can be useful if it is honest about what it controls, what it sources, and how it manages suppliers. The risk is not trading itself; the risk is hidden control.

### 2. Is a factory video enough to prove a supplier is real?

No. A video can provide useful visible signals, but it cannot prove everything about the supplier. Use it together with company details, quotation information, product-specific answers, and payment consistency.

### 3. What should I ask during a factory video call?

Ask to see areas connected to your product: machines, materials, semi-finished goods, sample room, packaging area, labels, or warehouse. Also ask which steps are done in-house and which steps are outsourced.

### 4. What is a red flag when a supplier claims to be a factory?

A common red flag is a supplier claiming to be a factory while selling many unrelated product categories and giving only generic answers. Another is refusing to explain company name, payment account, or production control clearly.

### 5. What can this check not prove?

It cannot prove future performance, payment safety, compliance, or order outcome. It only helps buyers compare visible and written signals before deciding what to ask next.
