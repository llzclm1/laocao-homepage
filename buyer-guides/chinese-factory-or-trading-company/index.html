Factory vs Trading

Is Your Chinese Supplier a Factory or Trading Company?

Learn practical ways to check whether a Chinese supplier may be a factory or trading company, including product range, workshop evidence, technical replies, and business consistency.

Not a formal factory audit. Not legal due diligence. Not quality inspection. Not a supplier reliability guarantee.

Short answer

The more useful question is not just “factory or trading company,” but whether the supplier is transparent about what they actually control.

Why this matters

Many buyers assume “factory” automatically means lower risk. That is too simple. A trading company can still manage a project well if the scope is clear and the process is transparent. A factory can still be a poor fit if the product is outside its real strength. What matters most is whether the supplier gives consistent, product-specific answers and makes responsibility boundaries visible.

Step-by-step checklist

1. Check whether the supplier’s product range feels focused or overly broad.

2. Ask which processes are done in-house and which are outsourced.

3. Compare workshop evidence with the claimed capability.

4. Compare technical reply quality with the complexity of your product.

5. Watch whether business identity and communication stay consistent.

Compare What the Supplier Actually Controls

Use specific responsibilities instead of relying on the label “factory” or “trading company.”

Product and sample control

Who explains materials, processes, tolerances, finishes, and functions? Who prepares the sample, approves changes, and records what should carry into bulk production?

Price and quotation control

Can the supplier explain what the price includes, what is excluded, and how quantity, material, tooling, customization, or packaging would change it?

Packaging and delivery control

Who confirms cartons, labels, logos, pallets, or retail packaging? Can the supplier explain what affects sample and production lead time?

Factory communication

Can the supplier provide current, product-specific workshop, process, packing, or sample materials and explain which production steps happen in-house or elsewhere?

Common red flags

Extremely broad catalogs can be one warning sign. Generic answers on process questions are another. If the supplier talks like a manufacturer but cannot explain production steps, packaging flow, or sample-to-bulk differences, that gap matters more than the label they use for themselves.

When Each Supplier Type May Fit

A direct factory may be useful when the order requires repeated technical discussion, sample changes, tight material or tolerance control, or direct access to the production schedule.

A trading company may be useful when the order covers several product categories, export packaging and documentation require coordination, or the buyer needs one team to communicate across multiple factories.

Transparency matters more than the label

Ask what the supplier controls, what another factory controls, and who handles sample, packaging, production, and quality communication when something changes.

Example supplier questions

Which process steps are done in-house?

Which steps are outsourced?

Can you show product-specific workshop evidence?

What part of this product category is your strongest area?

What this cannot prove

This cannot prove quality systems, legal standing, or future order reliability. It only helps clarify the supplier’s actual role.

Boundary

This guide helps assess business consistency, not formal audit, legal due diligence, quality inspection, or supplier reliability guarantee.
